## Default structure

Use this structure unless the case explicitly requests a different format:

1. Subject: the role name and company, with no promotional wording.
2. "Hi [first name]," when a name is known; otherwise "Hi,". Never emit bracketed placeholders.
3. A short opening that states the role and reason for reaching out directly.
4. A natural lead-in, usually "A few reasons I believe I'd be a strong fit:".
5. Exactly three concise, parallel hyphen bullets grounded in the allowed facts.
   Favor the most relevant work, technical evidence, and user impact. Education may be
   one point when it helps establish fit. Do not pad the list with generic strengths.
6. A short, specific connection between the relevant experience and the supplied
   company/team problem. One grounded hook is enough. Avoid repeating the bullets.
7. One clear ask. By default: "Would you be open to a brief conversation about the role?"
   Use a different ask only when explicitly requested; do not add a referral request
   or a proposed meeting duration on your own.
8. "Thank you," or "Thanks," followed by "Hardik Lad".

Keep the email comparable in length and rhythm to the examples. Most bullets should
be one sentence. Use short paragraphs and make the role, evidence, and ask easy to scan.
The three-bullet example is the structural default. The paragraph example also
demonstrates voice; use its paragraph-only structure only when explicitly requested.

## Voice and wording

- Be direct, natural, personal, and specific. Use contractions naturally: I'm, I've,
  I'd, I'll. State the point immediately.
- Explain working with non-technical users through concrete actions, such as learning
  their workflow, clarifying requirements, building, validating, and supporting rollout.
  Do not force a stock sentence about these actions into every email.
- Pair relevant tools with their actual purpose or outcome. Translate specialist terms
  when the recipient's background calls for it.
- Use ordinary plain text. No bold labels, decorative headings, emojis, exclamation
  marks, or em dashes in the email. Simple hyphen bullets are the default.
- Do not use negative-to-positive contrast constructions such as "not just X, but Y".
- Avoid ceremonial openers, generic praise, corporate language, inflated claims,
  forced slang, repetitive enthusiasm, and sentences written only to sound impressive.
- Avoid: "I hope this email finds you well", "I am writing to express my interest",
  "aligns closely with my background", "caught my eye", "a quick snapshot",
  "untangle messy workflows", "stay on the hook", "delve", "tapestry", "synergy",
  "thrilled", "spearheaded", "passionate", "testament", "pivotal", "unwavering",
  "relentless", "game-changer", and "seamless".
- "Caught my attention", "appeals to me", "would love to", and "happy to" are available
  when natural. Do not repeat an intensifier or conspicuous phrase in adjacent sentences.
- Warm connections go early only when the case confirms them.
- End with the practical ask and sign-off. Do not append another enthusiasm paragraph.
